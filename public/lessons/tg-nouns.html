The abstract door

This door is where most A1–A2 learners fail the "is it a noun?" test. Anchor with feelings + qualities: fun, fear, speed, power — all things Leo talks about with movies and soccer.

Quick check to ask: "Is fast a noun?" (No — it describes.) "Is speed a noun?" (Yes — it names.) That adjective/noun pair is the clearest contrast you can give.

Foreshadow uncountability but do not explain it yet.

Station 2 · How to RECOGNIZE a noun 🔍

Trick 1 — the "a / the" test 🎩

Put a or the in front. Does it sound OK? → It is a noun!

✔ Sounds good → NOUN

the coach 📣

✘ Sounds strange → NOT a noun

the happy 😀 … no!
a quickly 💨 … no!
the jump 🦘 … no!

The determiner test

This is the single most useful recognition tool for Leo, precisely because Japanese has no articles — practising the test also drills the article habit.

Honest caveat for you, not for Leo: "a run", "a jump" do exist as nouns in English. Keep the test at the level of "does it sound normal on its own?" If Leo notices, praise him hugely — he is thinking like a linguist.

🪑 Nouns also sit in two big chairs in a sentence:

Chair 1 (who does it): The goalkeeper caught the ball.

Chair 2 (what gets it): The goalkeeper caught the ball.

🧠 So: after the / a / my / this / two → look for a noun. And at the start and end of most sentences → also a noun!

Position beats meaning

This trick works even when Leo does not know the word. Try it: "The zorble caught the ball." He can tell you zorble is a noun purely from its seat. That is a genuinely thrilling moment for a 13-year-old — use it.

Do not use the words "subject" and "object" unless Leo asks. "Chair 1 / Chair 2" is enough at A1–A2.

Station 2 · Trick 4 · 日本語ブリッジ 🌉

Trick 4 — the "name" test 🏷️

Ask yourself one question: Is this word the NAME of something?

🇯🇵 In Japanese

名詞（めいし）＝ 名前のことば

ボール・スタジアム・友だち・じかん

These are all names → nouns ✔

🇬🇧 In English

ball · stadium · friend · time

Ask: does it NAME something?

YES → noun ✔    NO → not a noun ✘

🎒 Your 4-tool toolkit — say it with me:

1️⃣ Can I put a / the in front?   2️⃣ Can I count it?   3️⃣ Does it sit after the / my / this?   4️⃣ Does it name something?

Two YES answers = it is a noun. 🎯

The toolkit

Have Leo say the four tools out loud and count them on his fingers. This is the take-away he needs for the assessment at the end — everything after this is application.

Print tip: write the four tools on a sticky note for his desk. Recall beats re-teaching.

⚠️ Highest-value slide for a Japanese learner

Japanese has no capital letters at all. This rule does not exist in Leo's first language, so it will never be picked up by exposure — it must be taught explicitly and re-taught often.

The test to give him: "Is there only ONE in the whole world with this name?" → capital letter.

Expect this error for years. Correct it gently and consistently every single time it appears in his writing.

⚠️ The single biggest L1 gap

Japanese does not mark plural. 猫 is one cat or ten cats. So adding -s is not a spelling detail for Leo — it is a brand-new grammatical habit his brain has literally never needed.

Expect dropped plurals for years, even when he "knows" the rule. Do not treat it as carelessness. Correct warmly, every time, forever.

Naming the difficulty out loud (as this slide does) removes the shame and makes practice feel like training, not failure.

Machine 1 and Machine 2 ⚙️

👂 Listen! The -s has THREE sounds:

/s/ quiet → kicks, tickets  ·  /z/ noisy → goals, fans  ·  /ɪz/ extra beat → matches, boxes

🚫 Not "goal-su" — keep it short and smooth!

Machines + pronunciation

Soccer gives you matches and coaches for free — use them as the anchor examples for Machine 2.

Pronunciation is the Japanese-specific risk: Japanese is a mora language, so Leo will tend to insert a vowel — "goal-su", "kick-su". Model the clean consonant cluster and have him repeat 5×. This connects straight back to your Unit 9 Sound Lab work.

The /ɪz/ ending is easiest — it already sounds like a syllable to him.

Do not chase completeness — roofs, chiefs are exceptions Leo does not need yet.

Station 5 · Count or Can't Count 🔢💧

Players ⚽ and Water 💧

Some nouns you can count like players. Some are like water on the pitch — you can't count it!

⚽ COUNTABLE — like players

one goal · two goals 🥅
one ticket · three tickets 🎫
one player · eleven players 👕

✔ can take -s  ·  ✔ use many

💧 UNCOUNTABLE — like water

water 💧 · milk 🥛 · rice 🍚
money 💴 · time ⏰ · music 🎵
homework 📚 · information ℹ️

✘ never -s  ·  ✔ use much / some

🥤 Need to count water? Use a container!

a bottle of water 🍶  ·  a cup of tea ☕  ·  a bowl of rice 🍚  ·  a piece of advice 💬

⚠️ Second big L1 minefield

Japanese counts everything with counters (一本、二枚、三人). So "this noun cannot be counted" is genuinely alien — it is not laziness when Leo says "two waters".

The container structure (a bottle of…) is the bridge: it is exactly what Japanese counters do, so it feels natural once he sees the parallel. Point that out explicitly.

Keep the list short. These eight are the ones he will actually meet.

🌉 The one place L1 HELPS

English 's maps almost perfectly onto Japanese の — same meaning, same word order. Say this loudly; Leo has spent this whole deck learning things Japanese does not have, and this is a gift.

Do not over-teach s' (plural possessive). Recognition is enough at A1–A2; production comes later.

🎮 Game 9 · Key or Shortcut? 🔑✂️

Does 's mean OWNS 🔑 or IS ✂️?

The same little mark does two different jobs! Read the sentence and choose.

1️⃣ Leo's ball is new. ⚽
🔑 OWNS — it belongs to Leo
✂️ IS — Leo is…
2️⃣ Leo's very fast today! 💨
🔑 OWNS — it belongs to Leo
✂️ IS — Leo is very fast
3️⃣ The coach's plan worked. 📣
🔑 OWNS — the plan of the coach
✂️ IS — the coach is…
4️⃣ My dad's a teacher. 👨‍🏫
🔑 OWNS — it belongs to dad
✂️ IS — my dad is a teacher

🔍 The trick: if a noun comes straight after → OWNS 🔑. If a describing word or a/an comes after → IS ✂️.

The 's ambiguity

This confuses learners at every level. The rule in the gold box is reliable enough for A1–A2 and gives Leo something concrete to apply.

Say both sentence pairs aloud back-to-back — "Leo's ball / Leo's fast" — so he hears that the sound is identical and only the following word decides.
