This deck is the partner to Noun Stadium. Run it after nouns, not before — every station here assumes Leo can already find the noun.

The one big idea: a sentence needs a WHO and an ENGINE. If he can find those two, he can fix most of his own mistakes.

💡 Every word you just said is a VERB

run · kick · save · celebrate — these are the words that make things happen. Without them, a sentence just sits there. 🪑

Warm Up — production before rules

Do not say the word "verb" yet. Let Leo produce four verbs first, then name what he already did. Naming after doing sticks far better than naming before.

Accept "run" or "running" — both are right at this stage. Station 5 sorts out which to use when; correcting it now just teaches him to stay quiet.

Station 1 · The Big Idea ⚙️

A sentence needs a WHO and an ENGINE

The noun is the player. The verb is what the player does.

🏷️ WHO

Leo

the noun · the player

⚙️ ENGINE

kicks

the verb · what he does

🎯 WHAT

the ball

another noun

🚫 Take the engine away…

Leo ______ the ball. → Nothing happens. The sentence is broken. ⚠️
Every English sentence needs a verb. Every single one.

Station 1 — the frame for the whole deck

Come back to WHO → ENGINE → WHAT at every later station. It is the single organiser that makes Stations 4, 7 and 8 make sense.

Have Leo build three of his own on this frame before moving on — one about soccer, one about Batman, one about you. Production, not recognition.

🔍 The verb test — it works on ALL of them

Put I can ___ or Yesterday I ___ed in front of it.
I can kick ✔ · I can like ✔ · I can ball ✘ ← that one is a noun! 🏷️

Why quiet verbs matter

Leo's instinct will be "verb = action". That definition breaks on like, know, have — and on be, which is Station 3. Widen the definition now, cheaply, before BE arrives.

The "I can ___" test is the tool he keeps. Drill it here so it is automatic by Station 2.

Game 3 — the bridge into BE

The first two sentences are designed to be failed. Leo will hunt for an action, find none, and tap runner or fast. That confusion is exactly what makes Station 3 land.

Do not rescue him quickly. Let him sit in it, then say: "there IS an engine — it is just a very quiet one."

The last sentence needs two taps — one sentence can have more than one engine.

Station 3 · The Mirror Verb 🪞

am · is · are — the verb that does nothing

BE does not act. It is a mirror: it links WHO to WHAT or HOW. 〜です・〜います

Leo is a runner.
WHO = WHAT 🪞

The stadium is big.
WHO = HOW 🪞

The fans are here.
WHO = WHERE 🪞

🚨 The #1 mistake — never drop BE

I hungry. ❌   →   I am hungry. ✔
He fast. ❌   →   He is fast. ✔

Station 3 — the highest-value station in the deck

This is the Japanese-speaker error. 「おなかがすいた」carries no separate "am", and adjectives in Japanese already behave like verbs (たかい = "is tall"). So "I hungry" is not carelessness — his L1 genuinely does not need the word.

Expect this error for years. Correct it warmly every single time, and always by recasting ("I am hungry too!") rather than stopping him.

The mirror image is the metaphor to keep: an action verb DOES, a mirror verb EQUALS.

Station 4 · One person, one s ⚙️

He · She · It → the verb gets an -s

This is the rule Leo's ear will fight hardest. Say each line out loud. 🗣️

✅ No -s

I play soccer.
You play soccer.
We play soccer.
They play soccer.

⚙️ Add -s

He plays soccer.
She plays soccer.
It works.
Kubo plays for Real Sociedad.

🌉 Japanese bridge — why this is invisible to your ear

Japanese verbs never change for who is doing them. する is する for 私・あなた・彼. 🇯🇵
So this -s carries no meaning for Leo — it is pure habit. Habits are built by saying, not by knowing. 💪

Station 4 — the marathon rule

Third-person -s is the last thing learners master and the first thing they drop under pressure. Leo will "know" it and still omit it for years. That is completely normal.

Do not teach it as a spelling rule. Teach it as a sound Leo must produce, out loud, in full sentences. Ten spoken reps beat any worksheet.

Point out that it is the mirror image of nouns: -s on a NOUN means many; -s on a VERB means one. That symmetry genuinely helps.

Before he answers, make him point at the WHO. One person → one s. Many → no s. The pointing is the strategy you want to survive past today.

Station 5 · Two clocks ⏱️

Is it happening right now, or every day?

📅 EVERY DAY — a habit

He plays soccer on Saturdays.
I watch movies at home.

Signal words: every day · always · usually · on Saturdays

🔴 RIGHT NOW — live!

Look! He is playing right now!
I am watching the match.

Signal words: Look! · now · right now · at the moment

🌉 Japanese bridge 〜ている

「今 プレーしている」= be + ing. You already have this idea! 🇯🇵
The new part is that English needs two words: am / is / are AND -ing. Dropping the BE is the usual slip. ⚠️

Station 5 — lead with the signal words

Do not teach "present simple vs present continuous" as labels. Teach two clocks and the words that point to each. Leo picks the clock, the form follows.

The predictable error is "He playing now" — the -ing arrives, the BE goes missing. That is Station 3 resurfacing, so it is worth linking the two out loud.

〜ている is a genuine gift here. Use it: he is not learning a new concept, only a new packaging.

Priorities

Machine 1 covers most verbs — get that automatic. Machine 2 (drop the e) is the second priority because making, scoring, taking are everywhere.

The doubling rule is genuinely hard and low-yield. Introduce it, do not drill it. Running and swimming as memorised words will carry him further than the rule will.

👂 The -ed has THREE sounds — but only ONE spelling

/t/ → watched, kicked  ·  /d/ → played, saved  ·  /ɪd/ → wanted, shouted
🚫 Not "watch-ed-o" — keep it tight and short!

Station 6 — pronunciation is the Japanese-specific risk

Japanese is a mora language, so Leo will want to add a vowel: "watch-ed-o", "kick-ed-o". Model the clean cluster and have him repeat 5×. This is the same work as the -s endings in Sound Lab.

The three sounds are automatic for natives and invisible to him. Do not make him learn the three-way split — just make him copy it.

Station 7 · The seating plan 🪑

The engine sits in the middle

🏷️ WHO  →  ⚙️ ENGINE  →  🎯 WHAT

Leo    plays    soccer. ✔

❌ Wrong

Leo soccer plays.
Plays Leo soccer.

🌉 Japanese bridge — the BIG one

Japanese puts the verb LAST: レオは サッカーを する。🇯🇵
English puts it in the MIDDLE: Leo plays soccer. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿
This is why the word order feels backwards. It is not you — the languages really are built differently.

Station 7 — name the structural difference out loud

Japanese is SOV, English is SVO. Leo does not need those labels, but he absolutely needs to know the difference is real and expected. Otherwise he reads his own word-order errors as stupidity.

Whenever he produces a verb-final sentence, do not just correct it — point at the seating plan. Same frame, every time, all year.

Station 8 · The helper engine ❓

To ask a question, call a helper 🦸

The helper goes to the FRONT — and the main verb goes back to plain.

✅ Statement

He plays for Barcelona.
They watched the game.
You like soccer.

❓ Question

Does he play for Barcelona?
Did they watch the game?
Do you like soccer?

🔑 The golden rule — only ONE word carries the ending

Does he plays? ❌  →  Does he play? ✔
Did they watched? ❌  →  Did they watch? ✔
The helper takes the -s or the -ed. The main verb goes plain. 🪄

Station 8 — one rule, not two

Teach the one ending idea rather than separate question rules. Once Leo sees that does already carries the -s, "Does he plays?" stops feeling right to him.

This is also the pay-off for Station 4. If third-person -s is still shaky, do this station tomorrow rather than today.

🪞 BE is its own helper — it needs no Do

Is he playing? ✔  (not "Does he is playing?" ❌)
He isn't ready. ✔  ·  They aren't here. ✔
BE jumps to the front all by itself. It never asks Do for help.

Keep BE separate in his head

The classic confusion is mixing the two systems — "Does he is playing?" or "He doesn't be ready." Draw the line clearly: BE moves itself; every other verb calls Do.

doesn't is where third-person -s hides one more time. Point that out — it is the same rule wearing a different coat.
